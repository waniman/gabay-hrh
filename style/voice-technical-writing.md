## 1. Scope

- **Document types covered:** Analytical technical reports (country profiles), conceptual frameworks and discussion papers, policy commentaries, and recommendation sections ("Ways Forward").
- **Corpus:**
  - **PH (primary voice source):** *Human Resources for Health: Philippine Country Profile 2025* (working file). Capeding, Hesham, Ocampo Tan, Dayrit. About 25,500 words of prose analyzed.
  - **VN (reference model, country-profile genre):** *Human Resources for Health Country Profiles: Viet Nam* (WHO, 2016). Drafted by the Health Strategy and Policy Institute (HSPI).
  - **UHC (reference model, policy commentary):** *Implementing UHC in the Philippines: Is the Glass Half Full or Half Empty?* (ThinkWell, 2021). Nuevo, Yap, Boxshall, Ravishankar.
  - **WHO (reference model, conceptual framework):** *A Conceptual Framework for Action on the Social Determinants of Health* (WHO, 2010). Solar and Irwin. About 27,000 words of prose analyzed.
- **Primary readers:** Department of Health (DOH) bureaus, the Human Resources for Health (HRH) Network, World Health Organization (WHO) technical officers, national agencies (DBM, CSC, PRC, CHED), local government units (LGUs), development partners, and academic peers.
- **Not covered by this profile:** Facebook explainers, speeches, emails, journal manuscripts.

## 2. Voice Summary

Each paragraph states its point, supports it with a cited source or a disaggregated figure from a named dataset, then states what the evidence can and cannot show. Interpretation is cautious: limits of the data sit in the same paragraph as the finding. Concepts are explained in plain words before they are used, starting with the opening summary, and multi-part arguments are numbered inline. Recommendations name the responsible agency and the mechanism or data fields required.

## 4. Paragraph Architecture

- **How paragraphs open (RULE: depends on the section's job):**

| Section type | Opening move | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Data and analysis | The figure or ratio, with year and source | "NCR's HRH density is 4.1 times the density of BARMM (124.7 vs 30.4 per 10,000 population)..." (PH) |
| Summary and recommendation ("Ways Forward", executive summary) | A short verdict sentence in plain words, then the figure that proves it | "The HRH is young. In 2023, 30.0% of all health workers were aged 25–34..." (PH) |
| Conceptual or framework | A definition or claim, then its source | "This is a deliberately broad term that refers to the spectrum of factors in society that cannot be directly measured at the individual level." (WHO) |
| Context and background | A plain descriptive fact | "The Philippines is located between the South China Sea and the Pacific Ocean." (PH) |
| Policy commentary | The opportunity the policy creates, then the constraint, with evidence | "Data shows that for PhilHealth covered indigent families, only 53% knew of their entitlement for no balance billing..." (UHC) |

- **RULE: no term before it is explained.** A summary or verdict sentence may open a document or section, but it uses only words the reader already knows. Method labels, coined terms and operational shorthand appear only after the idea has been explained in plain words, and then in parentheses (e.g., "the legal limit on how much towns can spend on salaries (the PS cap)").
- **How paragraphs close:** With one of four moves: a limit on inference ("Thus, these figures do not represent the cumulative net loss of Philippine HRH." PH); a "should" recommendation; an implication for planning; or a link to a framework figure ("(see Figure B)" WHO).
- **How documents and chapters open:** State the questions the document answers. WHO opens with three numbered questions and maps each to a later section. PH opens chapters with context, then analysis.
- **How chapters close:** With a "Chapter Summary and Ways Forward" (PH) or a numbered list of key tasks (WHO: "A key task for the CSDH will be: 1... 2... 3...").
- **Transitions:** Sentence-initial *However,* (PH 19; WHO 17), *Moreover,*, *Similarly,*, *Thus,*, *Finally,*, *Meanwhile,*. UHC uses *Still,* and *Regrettably,* to turn from opportunity to constraint; prefer *However,* to keep the register neutral.

## 6. Vocabulary

- **Preferred terms:** cadre; stock; density per 10,000 population; maldistribution; licensed pool versus active stock; headcounts versus full-time equivalents (FTEs); pipeline; absorption; deployment; return service; regularization; plantilla positions; fiscal space; enumerate; screening indicators; complementary but non-additive (sources). From WHO: structural and intermediary determinants; social gradient; health equity; intersectoral action; entry points for action.
- **Habitual words to watch:** *ensure* (47 uses in PH) and *remains/persists* (29) read as filler when stacked. "The HRH" as a collective noun (47 uses) is a PH habit; use "the health workforce" in documents for non-specialist readers.
- **Terms rarely or never used:** delve, robust, pivotal, leverage, holistic, seamless, tapestry, foster, journey, realm (0 each in PH).
- **People-first conventions:** Use "people living in poverty" or "low-income households" in place of "the poor"; "persons with disabilities"; "indigenous peoples"; "communities affected by outbreaks". The corpus mostly describes health workers, so this convention rests on the standing rule.
- **Spelling and house style:** American English (program, -ize, labor), except proper nouns ("Labour Force Survey"). WHO style: Viet Nam, noncommunicable. Comma thousands separator (10,000). WHO and VN use British spelling (programme); do not copy it.
- **Number style:** Exact counts with commas (1,089,878); percentages to one or two decimals; 95% confidence intervals in parentheses; ranges with en dashes (2019–2023); "PhP 69.91 billion"; inequity as ratios ("4.1 times").
- **Acronym handling:** Spell out at first body use with the acronym in parentheses. PH uses about 63 acronyms five times or more, and some (LFS, PSA, NHWSS, KRA) appear without a body definition. Reduce acronym count for non-specialist readers.
- **Coined and method terms:** Explain the idea in plain words before the term first appears, never after. In summaries and openings, use the plain explanation and put the label in parentheses, or leave the label out.
- **Philippine-specific terms kept as is:** LGU, plantilla, barangay, barangay health worker (BHW), PhilHealth Konsulta, Magna Carta (for public health workers), Mandanas-Garcia ruling, Special Health Fund, Local Investment Plan for Health, health care provider network (HCPN), province-wide and city-wide health systems, return service agreement (RSA), region names (NCR, BARMM, CALABARZON, MIMAROPA).

## 7. Evidence and Citation Handling

- **RULE: cite almost every factual claim.** This includes claims that seem like common knowledge, figures from own analysis, laws, and policy issuances. The PH working file cites 32% of paragraphs; drafts should exceed WHO (46%) and UHC (56%).
- **In-text format:** Vancouver numbered superscripts placed after the punctuation mark; several sources separated by commas without spaces: "...tertiary education facilities.³²,⁵⁶" Numbers follow order of first appearance; a reused source keeps its first number.
- **Reference list:** Numbered, under the heading "References", in NLM/Vancouver form.
  - Journal: "Nisperos GA, Cabanizas TGG, et al. Implications of the Mandanas-Garcia Ruling on Local Health Systems. Acta Med Philipp. 2024 Jul 31;58(13). doi:10.47895/amp.v58i13.8131"
  - Web or grey literature: "Title [Internet]. Place: Publisher; Year [cited 2026 May 24]. Available from: URL"
  - Law: "Republic Act No. 11223: An Act Instituting Universal Health Care for All Filipinos... [Internet]. RA 11223. 2019. Available from: URL"
  - Dataset: "Philippine Statistics Authority. Labor Force Survey 2023 [dataset]. Quezon City: PSA; 2024."
- **Own analysis of datasets:** Name the dataset in the sentence, add its superscript, and repeat it in the source line under the table or figure: "Source: Analysis of PSA Labour Force Survey 2019–2023."
- **RULE: name the instrument (9 November 2026).** Any statement that an agency can, must or already does act names the policy instrument behind it, by type, number and issuer, and cites it. Laws are one kind; agency issuances, joint circulars, facility rules and accreditation standards are others. The claim stays within the instrument's scope and strength (`core/policy-instruments.md`). Example: "DOH can set both standards for its own hospitals through an Administrative Order (legal basis, Year)." An unnamed authority is an uncited claim.
- **Genre-specific tag (HRH country profile only):** Recommendations close with a link to the national plan objective, for example "(NHRHMP, 2020, KRA 1, SO2)". Do not use this tag in other document types.
- **Preferred source types and order of trust:** Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA) surveys and census; DOH administrative data; regulator data (PRC, CHED, TESDA, DMW, CFO, DBM); laws and issuances; national plans; WHO frameworks and guidance; peer-reviewed literature.
- **How conflicting figures are handled:** Report sources side by side, name the reason they differ ("coverage rather than measurement error"), state which source is used and why, and label overlapping sources "complementary but non-additive" (PH).
- **How disaggregation is reported:** By region, cadre, sector, age band, and sex, with 95% confidence intervals for survey estimates; extremes compared as ratios.

## 8. Tables, Figures, and Data Gaps

- **Table introduction:** Prose names the headline figure and cites the table in parentheses "(Table 19)" or as subject "Table 12 presents...". One or two comparisons follow; the reader reads the rest.
- **Captions:** Numbered, title case, placed above, stating measure, population, and years: "Table 22. Geographical Concentration of the HRH, in % (95% CI), 2023". Below: "Source:" line, then notes for abbreviations ("n.a. = not enumerated in the source.").
- **Framework figures (WHO):** Each conceptual figure is introduced in prose and referred back to as "(see Figure B)" where the text explains one of its components.
- **Data gaps:** Stated plainly in one sentence at the point of use, and distinguished from proof of absence: "a consolidated and publicly reported system has not been identified that tracks..." (PH). In indicator matrices: "Not available to authors of the paper."

## 9. Recommendations

- **Structure:** Bold verdict sentence; two or three cited figures; mechanism or limitation; "should" recommendation naming the agency or system. In HRH country profiles, add the plan tag (Section 7).
- **Typical verbs:** should enumerate, capture, inform, record, monitor, be analyzed, be tested, be established; "is the response required".
- **Numbered task lists:** For agenda-setting documents, close with numbered key tasks, each starting with an infinitive: "1 to identify successful examples of intersectoral action..." (WHO).
- **Level of specificity:** Name *who* (agency) and *what* (mechanism or data fields). The corpus never states *by when*; add a timeframe or trigger where the document type allows.

## 10. Anti-Patterns: Always Deleted

- **Em dashes (RULE: never use).** Rewrite as two sentences, or use a colon or parentheses.
- **Contrast framing.** Two kinds appear in the corpus:
  - *Distinguishing contrast:* separates two measures or concepts a reader would otherwise confuse. Example: "the LFS captures headcounts rather than full-time equivalents" (PH). This carries information.
  - *Rhetorical contrast:* sets up a weaker idea only to knock it down. Example: "not a bigger piece of the cake, but a different cake" (WHO); "a substitute for local hiring rather than a supplement to it" (PH). This adds emphasis but no information.
  - **RULE (confirmed 21 October 2026):** Keep distinguishing contrasts when the confusion is real and common. Rewrite rhetorical contrasts as an affirmative statement: "Deployment is functioning as a substitute for local hiring."
- **Terms before explanation (RULE, 8 November 2026).** No method label, coined term or operational shorthand (e.g., "passes Gate 1", "the problem to own", "check 3") appears before the idea is explained in plain words. This applies most of all to opening summaries, which Juhani finds the clearest sign of AI writing. Explain first; put the label in parentheses after.
- **Verdicts in place of evidence (RULE, 9 November 2026).** A phrase that states the result of a check without its evidence: "under authority DOH already holds", "within its mandate", "feasible and sustainable", "aligned with UHC", "a proven approach". Replace it with the named instrument (Section 7), figure or mechanism, with a citation. If the source is not in hand, write the data-gap sentence (Section 8) or a [SOURCE NEEDED] marker. Never soften an unsourced claim into abstract wording. Frameworks used to plan the document (mandate, feasibility, the person who can act on it) are thinking checks: the text carries what each check found, never its label or bare verdict.
- **Two jobs in one sentence (RULE, 9 November 2026).** A purpose, objective or opening sentence does one job: what will be done, for whom and by when. A trailing clause that carries a second argument moves to its own sentence in the section where the reader asks that question, or is cut. Example: "Purpose: To prepare, test and bring to DOH decision an anti-bullying policy and a structured residency rotation program... before June 2028, under authority DOH already holds." The last clause carries a separate argument (no new law is needed) as a vague verdict. The purpose line ends at "June 2028"; the legal basis goes to the adoption section, named and cited.
- **Groups of three adjectives.** Lists of three or more should be nouns (agencies, data fields), not adjectives.
- **Metaphor and rhetorical flourish.** Avoid "puts PHC center stage", "the glass half full" (UHC), "a credible bridge" (PH).
- **Aspirational language.** Avoid "deliver on the promise of UHC", "the vision is laudable" (UHC).
- **Evaluative adverbs.** Avoid *Regrettably*, *Arguably*, *Unfortunately* (UHC, WHO); state the finding.
- **Restating the same point twice** within a paragraph.
- **First-person plural** in reports ("we believe", "our answers", WHO). Use impersonal constructions.

Patterns common in AI writing that never or almost never appear in PH:

- Stock vocabulary: delve, robust, pivotal, leverage, holistic, seamless, tapestry, foster, realm, journey.
- Signposting openers: "It is important to note", "In conclusion".
- Opening summaries built on labels the reader has not met yet.
- Rhetorical questions in body text (WHO uses questions only to frame the paper's scope).
- Bolded inline labels followed by a colon inside bullet lists.
- Emojis, exclamation marks, and second-person address.
- Summary sentences that restate the paragraph's first sentence.
- Unattributed round numbers and uncited superlatives.

## 11. Exemplar Bank

All exemplars are under 25 words. Exemplars 1 to 5 are Juhani's (PH); 6 to 8 are from reference models he endorses.

### Exemplar 1 (PH)
> "The HRH is young. In 2023, 30.0% of all health workers were aged 25–34 and a further 6.7% were under 25..."

**Why it works:** A four-word verdict, then the figure that proves it, with year and precision.

### Exemplar 2 (PH)
> "Community health workers constitute a fifth of the HRH and are largely outside formal employment. The LFS estimated 216,940 community health workers in 2023..."

**Why it works:** The verdict carries both size and condition; the next sentence names the dataset as subject.

### Exemplar 3 (PH)
> "Return is also undocumented in both sources. Thus, these figures do not represent the cumulative net loss of Philippine HRH."

**Why it works:** States the data gap in seven words, then limits what the reader may conclude.

### Exemplar 4 (PH)
> "DOH establishes health-workforce standards, but a standard does not by itself create a funded, appointable position or determine compensation."

**Why it works:** Names the mechanism that breaks between policy and implementation in one plain sentence.

### Exemplar 5 (PH)
> "NCR's HRH density is 4.1 times the density of BARMM (124.7 vs 30.4 per 10,000 population)..."

**Why it works:** Expresses inequity as a ratio between named regions, with both raw values supplied.

### Exemplar 6 (WHO)
> "This is a deliberately broad term that refers to the spectrum of factors in society that cannot be directly measured at the individual level."

**Why it works:** Defines the scope of a concept before the argument uses it.

### Exemplar 7 (WHO)
> "These may be based on: (1) targeted programmes for disadvantaged populations; (2) closing health gaps between worse-off and better-off groups..."

**Why it works:** Numbers the parts of an argument inline so later sections can refer to each one.

### Exemplar 8 (UHC)
> "Data shows that for PhilHealth covered indigent families, only 53% knew of their entitlement for no balance billing..."

**Why it works:** Grounds a policy constraint in one survey figure from a cited source.

## 15. Uncertain Features

1. **Authorship of reference documents.** VN, UHC, and WHO are not authored by Juhani. Their features are adopted only where this profile says so.
2. **AI assistance and co-authorship in PH.** The "Ways Forward" sections are more uniform than the body chapters and may reflect AI-assisted drafting. Four authors are listed, so the fingerprint may blend voices.
3. **Pattern-based counts.** Passive, hedge, and citation counts may be off by several percentage points; WHO superscripts were detected from spaced numerals in the text layer.
4. **Bold lead sentences** were confirmed by font for one PH Ways Forward paragraph and inferred for the rest.
5. **Genre range.** No concept note, policy brief, or journal manuscript authored by Juhani is in the corpus.
6. **Drafting errors in PH** ("NCr", "the is a Master Plan objective") were treated as working-file artifacts, not voice.
